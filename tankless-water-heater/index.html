How it works

What a tankless water heater does differently

When a tap opens, a flow sensor starts the burner or the electric elements and water heats as it passes through a heat exchanger. The unit shuts off when the flow stops, which is why there is no standby loss.

The limit is the flow rate. A unit sized for one shower may struggle if a washing machine and a dishwasher run at the same time, so sizing by the number of fixtures matters more than the family size.

Installation

Gas, venting and sizing for a tankless unit

Gas tankless units draw a large gas load in a short time, so the existing gas line may need to be upsized. They also need a vent made for condensing or non-condensing exhaust, depending on the model.

Electric units can avoid venting but may need heavy circuits that an older panel cannot supply. We check the gas line, the vent route and the electrical panel before recommending a unit.

Flush and descale

Flushing and descaling a tankless heater

Minerals in water cling to the heat exchanger as scale. A flush circulates a descaling solution through the unit and clears it, which restores flow and lowers the chance of an error code.

Manufacturers commonly recommend a flush on a regular schedule, and hard water shortens the interval. Euless draws from the Trinity River Authority and city wells, and the mineral content of your own tap can vary, so a home test and the city's water quality report help set the schedule. A flush visit takes about an hour in most homes.

Repair

Error codes, cold sandwiches and fluctuating heat

Error codes usually point to an ignition fault, a vent problem, a flow sensor or scale. Water that goes hot and cold during a shower often means a flow rate that is too low for the burner to hold a steady temperature.

A plumber reads the code, checks the vent and the screens and then tests the sensor and igniter. A scaled heat exchanger is the most common cause of poor performance, and a flush often solves it.

Tradeoffs

Is tankless right for your house

Tankless units often last longer than tank heaters when they are maintained, and they free up floor space. They also cost more to install, and the savings depend on how much hot water you use.

If the existing heater is a failed tank, a swap to a new tank is often faster. A planned upgrade is the better time to move to tankless, as our installation page explains.
