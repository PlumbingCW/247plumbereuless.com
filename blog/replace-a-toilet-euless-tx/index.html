How to Replace a Toilet: A Euless, TX Homeowner Guide

Replacing a toilet is one of the more manageable plumbing jobs, and many homeowners do it themselves. It goes well when the measurements are right and the flange is sound, and it goes badly when a hidden problem shows up after the old toilet is lifted.

This guide walks through the steps, the checks that matter and the signs that mean it is time to call a plumber.

Before you buy

Measure the rough-in and pick the right toilet

The rough-in is the distance from the finished wall behind the toilet to the center of the drain flange bolts. Twelve inches is the most common, though ten and fourteen exist, and a toilet made for twelve will not fit a ten.

Also decide between round and elongated bowls, standard and comfort height and one-piece or two-piece designs. A dual-flush model saves water and adds a small mechanical part to maintain.

Removing the old toilet

Shut off, drain and lift

Turn the supply valve clockwise to shut off the water, flush to empty the tank and sponge out what remains. Disconnect the supply line, remove the nuts from the base bolts and rock the toilet gently to break the wax seal before lifting.

A toilet is heavy and awkward, so bend your knees and get a second person if you can. Set it on a towel or cardboard, and stuff a rag in the drain opening to block sewer gas while you work.

The flange

Inspect the flange before you set anything

The flange is the ring that holds the toilet to the floor. Check that it is solid, level with or slightly above the finished floor and free of cracks or rust.

A damaged flange is the main reason a toilet job turns into a plumber's job. A cracked or low flange cannot hold the new wax ring, and the toilet will leak at the base. Our toilet repair page explains the usual flange fixes.

Cracked or broken ring

The toilet cannot be held down evenly.

Flange below the floor

A wax ring cannot seal across the gap.

Rusted bolts or slots

Bolts cannot be tightened reliably.

Soft or stained floor

The subfloor may need repair first.

Setting the new toilet

Wax ring, bolts and leveling

1

Set the wax ring

Place the new ring on the flange or the bottom of the bowl.

2

Lower the toilet

Line up the bolts and press straight down.

3

Tighten evenly

Alternate sides until snug, and stop before porcelain cracks.

4

Connect and test

Connect the supply, fill the tank and check for leaks.

Do not rock the toilet after it is down, because the wax seals once and must be left in place. If the toilet is not level, shim it and caulk around the base after testing.

Testing

Leak testing and finishing

Fill the tank, flush several times and look for water at the base, the supply connection and the tank bolts. Wipe each joint with a dry tissue, which shows even a slow drip.

Check again after a day. A slow leak at the base can soak the floor over weeks, which is exactly the kind of damage that makes a failed seal a bigger problem than the toilet.

Tools

Tools and parts to have on hand

  • A new toilet, a new wax ring or gasket and new closet bolts.
  • An adjustable wrench, a putty knife and a small level.
  • Plumber's tape and a new supply line if the old one is stiff or corroded.
  • Rags, a bucket, a sponge and gloves.
  • Shims and a tube of bathroom caulk for finishing.

Gather everything before you shut off the water, because a trip to the store with the drain open and the old toilet out of the way is the most common way a one-hour job turns into a long evening.

If you are replacing a toilet on a second floor, move slowly and protect the floor and stairs. A toilet bowl weighs enough to damage tile if it is dropped, and a second pair of hands is worth having for the carry.

Mistakes

Mistakes that cause leaks after a swap

Overtightening the bolts is the classic error. Porcelain cracks under uneven force, so tighten each nut a little at a time and stop when the toilet is snug and level.

Reusing an old wax ring and skipping the leak test are the other two. A ring that has been compressed will not seal a second time, and a leak that is not found on day one soaks the floor for weeks.

When to call a plumber

Signs it is not a do-it-yourself job

Call a plumber if the flange is damaged, the floor is soft, the old toilet will not budge or you find water or sewage around the base. Also call if the drain is slow or the toilet backs up when other fixtures run, since that points to a bigger blockage.

For an urgent overflow, shut the supply valve and see our emergency toilet repair page. If other drains are backing up, sewer line cleaning may be the real fix.

Euless notes on sewer backups and who to call

If a toilet backs up along with other fixtures, the blockage may be in the city's main and not in your house. The City of Euless public works utility division takes wastewater backup reports on weekdays and has a separate line for after hours and weekends, according to its public works FAQ.

A plumber can run a camera on the private side to show whether the problem is yours. If the camera shows a clear line to the city connection, the city can be called with that information.
